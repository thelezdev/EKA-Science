Dinosaur fossil prices on the rise, a report on what it means for science

In a recent piece in the legacy science magazine Scientific American, journalist Emma Gometz probes into the emerging problem of rising costs of fossils, especially dinosaur fossils. The piece takes snippets from interviews with paleontologists, economists and people from the fossil trade and what this could mean for science.

A central pillar for the investigation is the recent auction of the Tyrannosaurus Rex specimen 'Sue', which sold for a whopping $50.1 million, also the most complete T-Rex specimen discovered (80% complete) . The report highlights how this may be bad for science because public institutions lack the funds to get fossils for so much. The report doesn't paint commercial fossil hunters in a bad light either, acknowledging that private collectors have loaned museums their collections for research and public display. However, private collectors may lack the facilities to care for the fossils in ways that may lead to valuable information being lost due to mishandling. Such practices may also be on the rise as an economist from the West Chester University interviewed in the report, Simon Condliffe, said that the fossils can be used as alternative assets as they are less volatile to market fluctuations than stocks. The concern of illegal activities like fossil poaching and digging on federal lands illegally is already being observed.

The primary concern seems to be that such practices don't just lead to barriers in research now but inflate the price of fossils of specific species from the point they are sold at. For instance, the Stegosaurus 'Apex' sold for $44 million, the second highest price for any fossil, and that not only means its Apex that is $44 million but that the price of any stegosaurus fossil from that point forth becomes $44 million, says Kirk Johnson, Sant director of the Smithsonian National Museum of Natural History. Auction bodies also set prices on metrics like how complete the fossil is. This can mean the most complete fossils will cost higher, making it harder for researchers to get a hold of the most valuable pieces.

A concerning trend that needs a lot more attention than the issue is currently getting.

Researchers at CalTech develop silicon chips with low signal loss comparable to optical fibers

Researchers at CalTech (California Institute of Technology) have achieved a significant feat of engineering by guiding light onto silicon wafers that has low signal loss comparable to that of Optical Fibers.

The research led by postdoctoral scholar Hao-Jing Chen and graduate student Kellan Colburn carried out their work in the lab of Ted and Ginger Jenkins Professor of Information Science and Technology and Applied Physics at Caltech, Kerry Vahala. The researchers create waveguides (nanoscale on-chip pathways that channel light) using germano-silicate, the same type of glass used in optical fiber. They adapt the material to a lithography-based manufacturing method suitable for making devices on wafers. The waveguides were arranged in spirals so they could have a much longer optical travel path while requiring much less area. The wafers were printed directly onto the 8-12inch wafers used for computer chips.

At near infrared wavelengths, the material matched the performance of some of the best contemporary devices made from silicon nitride. However, for visible light wavelengths, the new material far outpaced existing ones by a factor of 20, providing negligible signal loss comparable to optical fibers in the visible wavelengths.

The researchers say they still have room for improvements. The relatively low melting temperature of the new material allows the researchers to put the material in a furnace to 'reflow' the surface of their waveguides to get their smoothness down to the level of individual atoms, which largely suppresses the severe scattering loss that has limited conventional visible PICs. The researchers emphasize the need to test signal loss over metres and kilometres because while it may seem redundant, such measurements will show if the low signal loss can be retained over scales of metres and kilometres.

The researchers highlight immense practical potential for this new material. To demonstrate that versatility, the researchers describe several devices made with the new material in their paper. These include ring resonators, multiple types of lasers, and nonlinear resonators capable of generating a range of frequencies. The material could eventually find applications ranging from optical clocks and gyroscopes to quantum computers.

A worthwhile discovery.
