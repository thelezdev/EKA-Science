Researchers may have uncovered the brain region and processes necessary for entering hibernation in mammals

In a collaborative effort including the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, Whitehead Institute for biomedical research and the Harvard Medical School, Boston, researchers led by Adrian J Martinez and Christopher M Reid published a preprint on BioRxiv that is currently undergoing rigorous peer review. The study has identified the brain regions and specific nerve tissues responsible for entering a state of hibernation in mammals. While much is known about the physical and behavioral mechanisms of hibernation, the molecular markers and specific neuronal populations involved in entering a state of hibernation remain elusive. Previous studies have identified key regions involved in the characteristic low metabolism and low endothermia in mammals and torpor, a state of deep rest. The study wanted to identify the key regions necessary to enter a state of torpor. They monitored the Syrian hamster (Mesocricetus auratus), a seasonal hibernator, to check which brain regions were involved in aiding to enter such a state. They identified the anterior preoptic area (aPOA) in the hypothalamus as a core section involved in inducing torpor. They demonstrate Samd3-positive aPOA glutamatergic neurons are necessary for entry into hibernation and that their activation is sufficient to induce a prolonged hypothermic state with associated nesting behavior.

An interesting study worth following.
