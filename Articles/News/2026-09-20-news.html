Quantum entanglement measured in Z bosons at the LHC

In a massive experimental validation of quantum entanglement under extreme conditions, a huge group of scientists at CERN measured quantum entanglement in Z bosons that decayed from a Highs Boson.

In the Large Hadron Collider (LHC), located near the Swiss border, the group of researchers set out to test if quantum entanglement, the phenomenon where correlations between 2 or more particles cannot be independently inferred and are correlated, even across distances. Quantum entanglement is mostly understood as a fragile phenomenon that is produced in lab settings because of the difficulty in measuring this elusive phenomenon. However, it is a cornerstone of quantum mechanics and therefore occurs frequently in nature. Observing these phenomenon are difficult. Recent advances like entanglement measured in sunlight by researchers at Optica are steps in the right direction.

Professor Alan Barr, Oxford University, is a central figure in the study. He previously with another group has harnessed the capability of the LHC to measure entanglement in top quarks, the heaviest subatomic particles. His interest and innovation in measuring this fundamental quantum mechanics principle at high energies has been central in carrying out this experiment.

The Highs Boson, discovered at the LHC in 2012, is produced when protons traveling at 99.99% the speed of light collide at energies reaching thirteen trillion electron volts. The Higgs Boson decays into a pair of Z bosons which quickly, in fractions of seconds, decay into 4 leptons, into either electron or muons pairs. The researchers using the ATLAS experiment at CERN, could accurately measure the angles at which the Z bosons emerged before themselves decaying, from which they could infer the spin. The spin of both the Z bosons showed with strong statistical certainty that the pair of Z bosons were entangled (4.7 sigma, strong evidence).

This fundamental experiment is consistent with one of the core tenets of quantum mechanics under extremely violent conditions, only proving the robustness of the theory. A remarkable feat of experimental physics.

T-Rex may have had similar body temperatures to humans

Researchers at the University of California-Los Angeles, led by Randy Flores, measured the body temperature of a Tyrannosaurus Rex for the first time from teeth fragments.

The team had previously developed a prehistoric thermometer a decade ago but only improved efficiency enough to convince museums to donate irreplaceable fossils presently. The technology measures microscopic chemical bonds, rare isotopes of carbon and oxygen that can bond within the teeth enamel. The formation of such bonds partially depends on temperature as lower temperatures allow for more bonds to form. The specific measurements thus allows the paleontologists to infer body temperature as more bonds correlate with lower body temperatures and vice versa.

The team used two teeth from the specimen of Thomas the T-Rex donated by the Natural History Museum in Los Angeles. They used a dental drill to collect enamel from the fossil teeth. They then dissolved the powdered enamel in phosphoric acid, releasing carbon dioxide gas that contained the isotope bonds they wanted to study. Using a mass spectrometer to measure the isotope ratios, the team also pressurized the gas to create a denser stream of CO₂, which could then measure the ratios from smaller quantities of materials.

Teeth were used because skeletons dissolve throughout lives while teeth enamel remains more or less intact. The reading showed the T-Rex to have had 97°F or 36°C, very close to humans. This points towards a growing consensus of warm bloodedness and an active lifestyle in T-Rex. As a control, another specimen of a crocodilian from the same excavation site, the Hell Creek in Montana, made a reading of 86°F or 30°C. This is very significant not because a crocodilian as a reptile is not only cold blooded: If geology were to play a role, different specimens from the same sure would yield similar results. The difference validates that the readings are not because of chemical changes due to the environmental processes but biological.

This is remarkable because it may prove a precedent for further research on reconstructing Cretaceous and other prehistoric life more accurately. It also may point to the evolution of non-avian theropod dinosaurs away from cold bloodedness to body temperature regulation, giving a better explanation for discovery of T-Rex fossils in colder regions where other cold-blooded species' fossils from the same era are absent, as ectothermic reptiles would face greater physiological challenges. While aves have significantly higher body temperatures, it points to a trajectory of an active lifestyle and high metabolism in non-avian theropod dinosaurs.

An interesting finding worth following.
