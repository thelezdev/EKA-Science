Novel biomaterial aids better neural regeneration after ischemic strokes in mice

Biomedical Engineers at Duke University led by Shangjing Xin, who carried out their research in the lab of Tatiana Segura, also an author of the paper, have created a biomaterial that can help blood vessel growth and nerve regeneration in the brain after ischemic strokes in mice. Ischemic strokes happen due to blood clots in blood vessels; the event of such a stroke can lead to severe blood vessel and nervous damage. While after-treatment primarily rely on removal of these clots, dead or severely damaged blood vessels and nerves cannot be recovered. Rehabilitation only aids surviving vessels and nerves to adapt.

The researchers developed a biomaterial called microporous annealed particle scaffolds (MAPS) which consist of individual hydrogel microparticles that assemble into a porous structure. The open spaces provide cells with a framework they can enter and use while rebuilding neural tissue. The researchers further wanted to optimize the body's immune system and therefore used extracellular vesicles (EVs) from astrocytes, star-shaped cells which play important roles in normal brain function and respond quickly when the brain is injured. The researchers collected EVs from lab-grown rat astrocytes, activated them with cytokine combinations and tested them with different signaling molecules designed to attract immune cells, encourage blood vessel repair and improve function. Instead of simply injecting the EVs into the damaged area, the scientists chemically attached them to the surfaces of the hydrogel microparticles. Doing so kept the signals concentrated within the scaffold, giving incoming cells a better opportunity to encounter them. IL-4 and C1q were particularly effective at drawing potentially helpful immune cells into the injured region. Those cells included macrophages as well as a persistent population of neutrophils. Neutrophils are largely understood to cause inflammation and tissue damage in the earlier stages of strokes. However removing the neutrophil-rich immune-cell populations at later stages revealed their important role because doing so declined the formation of blood vessels significantly and the scaffold underwent less remodeling.

Mice who had received the biomaterial intervention were able to recover substantially in 8 weeks and showed performance similar to healthy mice in grid walking tests designed to test forelimb placement accuracy. The researchers observed more axonal fibers both inside and around the injured region, essential structures that allow brain cells to transmit signals. The researchers also tested if the EV from astrocytes alone produced similar results, the conclusion was that the MAPS biomaterial was required to create the scaffold, an environment that promotes better immune communication and subsequent better blood vessel and nervous regeneration.

The next step is to test these findings in human populations and investigate iPSC derived astrocyte EVs from humans, since the goal of this study was to find better treatments for strokes in humans. While preliminary, the study offers new perspectives on the nature of neutrophils and the wider role of the innate immune system in the aftermath of a stroke. And the results with mice populations are certainly hopeful.

A study worth keeping your eye on.

Source:

Published in the journal Cell Biomaterials, 21 July 2026: IL-4/C1q activated astrocyte-derived extracellular vesicles promote stroke infarct recovery by recruiting peripheral leukocytes

Genetic diversity of mosquitoes in India are driven more by geographical differences and less by climate

Researchers from various departments of the ICMR in Madhya Pradesh and Gurukula Kangri (Deemed to be) University, Uttarakhand led by Gaurav Sharma took to mapping the genetic diversity of aedes aegypti, the primary vector of dengue, chikungunya, Zika, and yellow fever, due to the species' high genetic plasticity across populations that can make different groups resistant to insecticides and also improve vector competence.

The study used partial mitochondrial cytochrome oxidase I (COI) gene sequences across 13 distinct geographical regions in 126 individuals. The mitochondrial sequencing revealed 21 haplotypes with moderate haplotype diversity and low nucleotide diversity. While region specific haplotypes indicated localized differentiations, the haplotype Hap_2 shared across all populations in a star-like haplotype network suggests a widespread ancestral lineage with signs of recent expansion. Genetic variation was highest in population pairs in Itanagar, Kota and Thiruvananthapuram. Various statistical techniques thereafter highlighted geographical differences to be a more significant predictor for these changes and not climate.

The researchers suspect geographical differences that allow populations to remain isolated from another to be the primary cause of such diversity and variation. Ther findings therefore suggest regional variants therefore be accounted for in tackling and intervening for vector-borne diseases.
