Washington University of Medicine researchers uncover immune structures surrounding the brain

Researchers at Washington University School of Medicine in St. Louis have uncovered that contrary to contemporary consensus, experiments revealed that the mice brain does have specialized lymph-node like immune structures in the skulls bone marrow.

The finding also found evidence of such immune structures in human skull bone marrow. In an earlier paper by the same researchers, the team led by Jonathan Kipnis, PhD, the Alan A. and Edith L. Wolff Distinguished Professor of Pathology & Immunology and a BJC Investigator at WashU Medicine, discovered lymphatic vessels in the dura mater, the thick, protective outer tissue layer that sits right between the skull and the brain. Their recent work found tiny physical channels bridging the skull, dura mater and brain tissue, channels through which immune cells and cellular waste can travel between the brain and nearby skull bone marrow.

Their recent study in mice followed proteins as they moved out of the brain, through these channels, and into the skull bone marrow where organized immune structures resembling those normally found in lymph nodes were discovered.

What was unexpected was precisely the discovery of the brain coordinating and even having specialized lymph-node like immune structures, which serve as important coordination centers for immune activity. Within them, T follicular helper cells support B cells, which can then produce large quantities of antibodies that help the body fight infections and disease.

Going a step further, the researchers interfered with mice brain with a model of glioblastoma specific to the species, an aggressive form of brain cancer, and interfered with the skull immune hubs using a drug. Mice with impaired immune tissues were found to grow tumors quicker than healthy mice. In a quest to find ways to target alterations to strengthen the immune structures, the researchers combined 3 immune-boosting proteins in gels and placed it directly beneath the scalp. This triggered an immune response that appread first in the skull bone marrow immune hubs and later in nearby lymph-like nodes outside the skull. Mice that received the gel had a higher chance of survival from this severe type of brain cancer than the control.

The study fundamentally changes the current paradigms of brain immunology. It may also find use in cancer treatment as researchers get to the point where they better understand and eventually experiment with the cause and effect relationship of the immune structures surrounding the brain.

A brilliant discovery.
