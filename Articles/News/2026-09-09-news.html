The microbiome may be more population diverse and readily contagious across continents than previously thought

A study led by the University of Vienna with a collaborator from the University of Veterinary Medicine Vienna concluded that the human gut microbiome may be a lot more diverse within species and also be more readily contagious than previous analyses have shown, with particularly competitive populations expanding across continents in only decades, a pattern previously observed only in pathogens.

The study examined thousands of bacterial isolates collected from the human gut, along with large amounts of metagenomic data from people in multiple countries and across different age and health groups. Metagenomic data includes the complete genetic information of microbial communities in the sample. To analyze these data, the researchers developed a bioinformatic method based on 'reverse ecology,' an approach that uses genomic information to infer how organisms have adapted to particular environments.

The analyses showed several populations developed distinct advantages that allowed them to have better survival chances in specific gut niches, a phenomenon termed genome-wide selective sweeps, which occur when an individual gains a beneficial mutation that gives it an advantage over closely related individuals, allowing its descendants to become dominant.

The team's analyses also revealed that the microbiome is a lot more contagious than previously thought, with competitive populations expanding across continents in only decades. Populations with superior adaptations also spread internationally and occupy more ecological niches.

The study leader Martin F. Polz from the University of Vienna stresses that understanding the complexity of the microbiome at the species level is clearly insufficient and populations must be understood moving forward. The authors state this can become the catalyst for the development of more precise drugs targeting niche populations and more similar functional populations.

This is a genuinely incremental development in understanding the microbiome, worth following.
