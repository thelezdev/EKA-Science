Ocean temperatures this August soared to highest at 21.10°C since recording began in 1979

The world's ocean temperature's have hit a record high of an average of 21.10°C on August 22, according to the European Union’s Copernicus Climate Change Service (C3S) for the extra-polar global ocean, 60°S–60°N, since the body started recording ocean temperatures since 1979. This is also significant because ocean temperatures are usually highest around March-April and are generally cooler around August. The C3S has identified this as not an isolated event but due to long term human activity and the ongoing El Niño. This is highly concerning and according to climate researchers "Not a surprising finding". The previous highest record was set in March of 2024 at 21.09°C. What is more worrying is that the record is anticipated to be broken in the coming months. This has devastating effects for already sensitive ocean habitats like corals and reefs and poses greater risks for coastal communities.

Preliminary experiments show mitochondria transplantation may help treat damaged optic nerve cells

Researchers at the Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai in New York City led by David Putrino published a preprint awaiting peer review for a case study of a woman who had suffered damage to her optic nerves from a brain bleed severe enough to cause near complete blindness which no response in her pupils from light. The researchers in March of this year after obtaining an emergency approval from the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA), had taken mitochondria, essentially called the 'power house of the cell', organelles involved centrally in cellular energy metabolism, from the patient's legs and transplanted them into her eyes intravitreally to help with recovery of her retinal ganglion cells. Transplanting mitochondria as a method to treat damamged organelles has already been explored for some time and has been prileviously done with heart and brain. This findings reported showed the injection of the mitochondria into the eyes was relatively safe and caused no adverse effects, including no immune system response like inflammation or damage. The patient's pupil reactivity “held at levels that were higher than they were prior to the transplant,” says Putrino. The researchers also measured the patient's neural activity in the brain using electrical methods, monitored at 27 and 4 days respectively before the procedure and at 3, 44 and 45 days respectively after. They found considerable neural activity in the patient's visual cortex before undertaking the procedure. Although most helpful symptoms were temporary and faded by the fourth week.

The researchers acknowledge the limitation of their findings. They don't attribute the improvement in pupil reactivity to the mitochondria transplantation as the study lacked the scope to establish rigorous causality. It is still however an interesting development and a larger study can explore the questions posed by this novel, experimental method
