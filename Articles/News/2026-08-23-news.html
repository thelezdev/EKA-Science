Tree Selection in Urban Areas is Key as Select Trees Worsen Urban Air Quality

Researchers at the Jinan University in Guangzhou, China led by Bin Yuan made a startling discovery that even the researchers initially found hard to believe. The team had set out to understand how human activity impacts ozone pollution; ozone can inflame and damage airways causing lung damage in susceptible individuals like people with asthma. The researchers collected air samples across Beijing between May and June 2021 to measure the concentration of volatile organic compound (VOC) emissions, released by some vegetation, vehicles and chemical industries. Ozone forms when VOCs react with hydroxyls in the atmosphere to form peroxy radicals, which in turn react with nitrogen oxides (NOx), which are mainly a byproduct of traffic and industries, in that presence of sunlight. The study measured how fast the emitted VOCs reacted with atmospheric hydroxyls to establish the contribution to Ozone formation. The study found that trees in Beijing produced roughly 10% of VOCs, mostly isoprene which is a type of tropane VOC, and the remaining emissions were human based. However, a staggering 52% of the VOCs that led to the formation of Ozone came from trees, VOCs produced by human activities mostly didn't convert to Ozone. The researchers found that temperature exacerbates the problem as hydroxyl reactions with VOCs at 35° jumped seven times more than at 20°C in the same trees. The study further analyzed the trees planted at 24 major cities to look for trees emitting isoprenes; cities like Sydney and Melbourne for instance have far higher isoprene emissions, at 65%, as compared to Beijing.

The study is a remarkable reminder of the role of science in dispelling assumptions. Urban plants, often selected for their capacity to sustain stress and grow fast, will need very careful selection. This study becomes a precedent to study the plants that may be compatible with modern megacities and inform 'Greening' initiatives in urban planning.
