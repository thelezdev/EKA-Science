Computer Scientists make a Major Breakthrough in a 30 year old Combinatorics Problem

Combinatorics can broadly be understood as the area of mathematics dealing with discrete objects and grouping them in separate groups so each group has more or less the same properties as the other. Discrepancy is the 'gap' between the properties of each group, as perfect distribution in almost all complex cases involving numerous objects and dimensions are impossible.

In a paper presented at the 58th Annual ACM Symposium on Theory of Computing (STOC 2026) in June of this year, researchers Haotian Jiang from the University of Chicago and Nikhil Bansal from the University of Michigan report a major breakthrough in Combinatorics. The Komlós conjecture has been an unproven counterintuitive conjecture that asserts that for any number of objects or dimensions, the discrepancy between 2 groups will not exceed a certain constant discrepancy independent of the number of dimensions or objects and the objects can be arranged as such that the discrepancy remains beyond that threshold. The conjecture was mostly assumed to be false as progress had stalled by the late 90s when in 1998 Wojciech Banaszczyk improved the bound of the discrepancy to the square root of log N (√log N) for N number of vectors. The researchers implemented the computational methods from Bansal's previous work when he, Jiang and three other researchers had made a computational algorithm that could limit the discrepancy of models to the √log N as in Banaszczyk's theory. They had assigned half a vector to each group and randomly massaged the two groups so one of them ended up with the full vector. For this new study, they used the same method but instead of randomly massaging the vectors so anyone ends up with the full vector, they looked at another factor, dependency, which is the change in the discrepancy of other dimensions as a result of a perturbation in one. As they carefully reduced this joint impact, they found a limit that changes significantly slower with the increase in dimensions and found an upper bound on the discrepancy as the fourth root of log N (4√log N), a number that grows so slowly compared to previous bounds that it gives better hope for finding a constant value. The researchers acknowledge that they haven't stumbled across a constant yet; Joel Spielman commented that constants rarely are the fourth roots of log N. Spielman had made an advancement to the conjecture earlier in 1985 and has shown excitement for this new discovery.

However, this has been a major advancement in the conjecture that has been stalled since the late 90s. It is a major breakthrough that holds promise for a future proof about the Komlós conjecture.

Researchers have imaged the Wavefunction of a Quantum Molecule with High Resolution Details

Researchers at the University of Göttingen led by Dr. Wiebke Bennecke have made significant strides in quantum tomography by imaging the 3D molecular orbital wavefunction of a nanometers long molecule. The researchers used a two step method. Photoelectron spectroscopy was first used to measure the photoelectron angular distribution and energy/momentum of the electrons emitted by the molecule using a novel method: by using powerful, lab-based soft-X-Ray that provides ultrashort light pulses instead of the contemporary measurements done at synchrotron research facilities. They then came up with a new computational algorithm that requires significantly less experimental data to produce reliable 3D images.

This has allowed the researchers to not only capture a high resolution image of the molecular orbital wavefunction of the molecule using significantly less experimental data but also may be a precedent for imaging dynamical molecular wavefunctions to capture how changes in energy, response to light, electronic processes and chemical reactions at the femtosecond timescales. A truly promising study worth following.

Spatial Proximity of Domain Relevant Objects may influence perceptions of Expertise and even factor in for better Persuasion

Researchers from the Helmut Schmidt University, Hamburg, Germany led by Scarlett Friedrich demonstrated that objects related to specific fields and disciplines and their spatial proximity to people may link to perceived domain specific expertise and even persuasion. The paper included 4 separate experiments. Participants in the first study attributed more expertise to people depicted with domain related objects vs without. In the second study depicting multiple individuals, participants ascribed better expertise to individuals spatially proximal to the domain relevant objects. A bi-directional relationship was established in the third study where participants, asked to place objects next to depicted individuals, placed the domain relevant objects closer to those described as experts. Study 4 demonstrated persuasive power of the proximity of the objects as communicators positioned closely to the objects elicited greater changes in attitude than those placed distally.

The study provides significant empirical evidence across the 4 experiments to make its claims and finds evidence for the importance of relevant objects and their use by people to distinguish their authority and their role in persuasuion.
