New study hints depression may not cause hippocampus shrinkage as previous analyses suggest

Researchers at the Washington University School of Medicine led by Kassandra Miyoko Hamilton and Xiaoke Luo analyzed brain scans from 23,417 participants in the US and the UK across six large-scale datasets, including the UK Biobank, Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) study and the Human Connectome Project. The study aimed to establish a more comprehensive analysis of the neural correlates of depression across the entire brain, seeking to resolve the literature gap in the neuroimaging of depression as previous studies often focus on subcortical and cortical brain regions. Worth mentioning is that the participants were not screened based on Major Depressive Disorder but looser depression symptoms and feelings broadly.The meta-analyses found no significant hippocampus shrinkage as stated by previous studies. The study also, surprisingly, found no significant structural changes in other subcortical regions. The study reported significant grey matter reductions in the prefrontal cortex (important in judgment and execution), the anterior cingulate (decision making and motivation) and the insula (processing pain) and other cortical regions, though less significantly. These may account for the brain fog, low motivation and executive dysregulation in depression, though the study does not establish a direct causal link to these or any other experienced symptoms. Alongside, structural associations of the visual and somatomotor networks in depression were confirmed as reported by recent developments in the field.

The researchers stressed that the lack of evidence for hippocampus shrinkage maybe due to the longitudial nature of previous studies that report these findings, as the meta-analysis did not analyze participants' data across time. However, the study did find a non-linear shrinkage in hippocampus volume in aging coherts and also acknowledge that shrinkage in the region may occur in severe cases of depression as most reports in their analysis consisted of participants reporting mild to moderate symptoms and more research needs to be done to validate the non-linear findings. The researchers also emphasized the neural correlates of depression were more consistent with self-reported questionnaires as opposed to screening depression through depressive traits in behavior like neurotism.
