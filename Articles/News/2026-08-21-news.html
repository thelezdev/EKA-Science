A Scientific Dispute Over the Presence of Key Immune Vessels in Bones

In 2023, a research team led by Anjali Kasumbe (then at the University of Oxford, now at now at Nanyang Technological University Singapore) published a paper that challenged a previously held assumption that while lymphatic vessels occur along the edges of healthy bones, they don't occur inside bones with exceptions in rare conditions such as Gorham-Stout disease. Using 3D imaging and prevalence of genetic markers in Lymphatic Endothlial Cells (LECs) that line Lymphatic vessels, the researchers were able to find the vessels in the bones of healthy mice and subsequently in humans. They also introduced toxins that targetted the LECs and their destruction led to the mice unable to recover from radiation-induced bone injuries, highlighting their importance. The work, according to Kasumbe was paradigm shifting. However, Kasumbe's former Post-doc adviser, Ralf Adams at the Max Planck Institute for Molecular Biomedicine, has been skeptical with the work and now with his collegues and cell biologist Bin Zhou at the Chinese Academy of Sciences, set out to replicate the findings and concluded that Kasumbe's methods had been flawed, the genetic markers could point to other structures and the toxins might have targetted other key structures too leading to general deterioration of bone health in the mice. Kasumbe retaliated, that the critics' methods were flawed. Researchers unaffiliated with the work hold that the criticism is robust and holds merit. The journal, Cell, which would have published both pieces of work, the criticism and counter-criticism, of the paper of 15 July, halted the publications a day prior, given the prevalence of broader ceriticism of Adams' work in Kasumbe's criticism; the decision has been said to be "Concerning" by Kasumbe.

Regardless of what becomes of the situation, one thing is clear, this will likely produce better science and clear up the issues surrounding the presence of Lymphatic tubes in bones.

Fossilized Footprint Tracks Suggest Mammalian Ancestors in the Age-of-Dinosaurs May Have Been A Lot Larger Than Previously Thought

A study led by Peter Buck from the Federal University of Rio Grande do Sul, analyzed new tracks, that were uncovered in the Botucatu Formation in southern Brazil, a site rich in fossil footprints but lacking in fossilized skeletons, uncovered footprints of a mammal or mammaliaform more than 10 centimeters long and 13 centimeters wide, roughly twice the size of any other mammalian tracks known from the formation. The fossil dates back 130 million years old in what would have been the Cretaceous super continent Gondwana. If supported by further evidence, this can enormously change our understanding of mammalian evolution and their role in the Mesozoic period.
