Clinical depression may stifle the growth of new neurons in the hippocampus

Researchers at the Department of Psychiatry in Columbia University, New York, USA sequenced almost 500,000 nuclei in the hippocampus of 30 individuals post mortem primarily through single-nucleus RNA sequencing (SnRNA-seq). 11 individuals at the time of death has Major Depressive Disorder (MDD) and the other 19 had no known mental health conditions, serving as a control group. The analysis probed the gene activity across the tissues and looked for markers of neural development trajectories: neural stem cells becoming intermediate neural progenitor cells, then developing into the precursors to neurons, known as neuroblasts, and finally into immature neurons. The study revealed MDD individuals didn't have an absence of neurogenesis but rather had an abundance if stem cells and not neuroblasts as compared to the control, pointing towards the possibility that neurogenesis may be halted due to depression. It remains unclear if stifled neurogenesis causes clinical depression or the other way around or if both of these factors stem from some underlying issue.

Additionally, the researchers had also conducted spatial transcriptomics and proteomics, to map active genes in the brain and the proteins in each tissue respectively. Experts in the field have pointed out the significance of the study, praising it for its wealth and depth of data. It also provided further evidence for neurogenesis in adults, a heavily debated topic, giving proponents a very strong foothold. The study can also lead to the potential synthesis of drugs that helps neurogenesis as the study implies to have found 'druggable pathways' which can help in psychiatric treatments for MDD.

Water treatment tank that removes arsenic and fluoride developed at IIT Guwahati

Researchers at the Indian Institute of Technology, Guwahati (IIT-G), Prof Mihir K. Purkait and Mr. Mukesh Bharti, reported developing a low cost water treatment tank that can remove both arsenic and fluoride simultaneously in an announcement on 25 August through the university's official press release. Conventional treatment methods don't target both elements together and treatments compete for removal sites. Rather than using a traditional aluminium electrode, the researchers used a rotating anode that constantly improves the mixing of compounds inside the reactor, enhances mass transfer, renews the electrode surface and promotes the formation of pollutant-capturing aluminium hydroxide flocs. When electricity passes through the reactor, aluminium ions and hydroxide ions combine to form microscopic flocs that bind with arsenic and fluoride, allowing both contaminants to be removed through adsorption, coagulation, and precipitation. Depending on the concentration under the tested conditions, the reactor achieved up to 98.2% arsenate and 91.8% fluoride removal within minutes. The initial demonstration was performed at an estimated operating cost of ₹18–₹58 per 1,000 litres of treated water.

The researchers varied rotational speed, current density, electrode spacing and treatment time, and importantly, they tested the technology against groundwater chemistry containing naturally occurring calcium, magnesium, bicarbonate, sulphate and phosphate, as well as real groundwater samples from Assam. This makes it a promising study worth following to see how practical realities are tackled.
