Axon's discovered to be more like strings of pearls rather than smooth tubes

In a series of collaborative studies with Shigeki Watanabe, Ph.D., an associate professor of cell biology and neuroscience at the Johns Hopkins University School of Medicine as a central figure, researchers have discovered the presence of much smaller, repeating swellings in axons examined under conditions intended to preserve their normal structure. This came as a massive surprise since axons have long been understood as smooth tubes with few bulges called synaptic varicosities usually found at nerve terminals where neurotransmitters are released and/or taken up.

The researchers called these regions "non-synaptic varicosities." Beaded axons had previously been observed only in dying neurons and in neurodegenerative conditions. Watanabe had first observed pearl-like structures in the axons of worms. He collaborated with Swiss scientist Graham Knott, Ph.D., Jacqueline Griswold, a Johns Hopkins graduate student and the study's first author and others to test whether the supporting network of proteins in axons had something to do with the pearls. Disrupting that framework had no effect on the presence of pearls.

The team examined neurons from adult mice and mice embryos in the laboratory using high pressure freezing electron microscopy to preserve their delicate shapes before examining them with beams of electrons. The tissues were non-myelinated. Across tens of thousands of tissue images, the pearl-like structures persisted. High resolution imaging revealed pearling in living neurons.

The researchers went further to test what influences the structure of the pearling. Mathematical models of the membrane surrounding the axons reproduced important features of the pearls, suggesting that physical forces could help determine the shape without requiring a rigid internal mold. Experiments supported that interpretation. Increasing the sugar concentration in the solution surrounding the axons made the swollen regions smaller. In the model, increasing membrane tension also reduced their size. Cholesterol provided another way to change the membrane's behavior, removing it made the membrane less stiff and more fluid.

The researchers also tested if electrical activity had an influence on the pearly structures and found after high frequency electrical stimulation, the pearl-like regions became an average of 8% longer and 17% wider, with enlargement lasting at least 30 minutes. The published paper reports that electrical signals slowed after this stimulation, with the effect lasting at least an hour.

Going even further, the team planned to examine axonal "arms" in human brain tissue obtained with permission from people undergoing brain surgery and from people who had died with neurodegenerative diseases. The researchers combined electrical stimulation with rapid freezing to capture tiny membrane changes in mouse and human brain slices. Alongside the pearled axons, they found evidence of ultrafast endocytosis, a process through which nerve endings quickly retrieve membrane after releasing chemical messages.

These do not establish if the pearl structures behave similarly in humans as in mice but one thing is clear, this challenges the simplicity of current the axonal concept. Watanabe has even said "These findings challenge a century of understanding about axon structure". More work needs to be done to better understand what influences the dimensions of these pearls and also how they relate to the beads observed in damaged neurons and neurodegenerative conditions.

But regardless, this is a brilliant development that is, rightfully, a breakthrough.
